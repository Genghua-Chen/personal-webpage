Are your points actually beating
a boring 2% cash back card?

A points card only wins when multiplier × cents-per-point > your cash back rate. Everything else — the annual fee, the transfer partners, the hotel award chart — is just detail feeding those two numbers. This page works out both: what Amex Gold and the Chase Sapphire cards earn on your real spending, and what a point is actually worth once you redeem it at Hyatt, Marriott, Hilton or IHG.

What a point is worth to you

In cents per point (cpp). This is the single most important assumption on the page, and it is personal — a point is worth exactly what you can redeem it for, not what a blog says.

The guaranteed alternative — cashing out
These two numbers are not the same thing, and mixing them up breaks the whole calculation. The transfer valuations above are a hope — you only get 1.7¢ out of an MR point if you actually transfer it to a partner and book something good. The cash-out rate is a fact: Amex will hand you about $600 for 100,000 MR, Chase about $1,000 for 100,000 UR, today, no effort. That asymmetry — Chase's floor being roughly 1.7× Amex's — is the most underrated difference between the two currencies.

Transfer partners — the structural fact

Only Chase reaches Hyatt. World of Hyatt is the last major program still running a published award chart, which is exactly why it holds ~2¢ of value while the dynamic programs drift toward the cash price. Amex has no Hyatt partnership at any ratio, so Chase UR has a materially higher realistic ceiling for hotels.

Amex → Hilton is a trap ratio. 1:2 looks generous until you price it: 2 Hilton points at ~0.5¢ is 1.0¢ of value for 1 MR you could have transferred to an airline at 1.8¢. Hilton points are best earned, not bought with transferable currency.

Marriott takes both at 1:1, and at ~0.7¢ that is a mediocre use of either. Marriott points are worth transferring into mainly when there is a transfer bonus running and a specific expensive property in mind.

Net: MR is an airline currency that happens to have hotel partners. UR is the one that does hotels well, entirely because of Hyatt.

Three rules that survive every program change

1 — Price the points you spend at what you would otherwise get, not at what you hope to get. This is the mistake that makes every hotel award look terrible. If you charge a burned MR point at 1.7¢ — the value of a great airline transfer you have not made and might never make — almost nothing clears the bar. The real alternative is concrete: Amex hands you $600 for 100,000 MR, Chase $1,000 for 100,000 UR. Subtract that.

The Hilton example makes it obvious. A night costing 95,000 Hilton points is only 0.41¢ per hotel point, which looks awful. But it came from 47,500 MR, and cashing those out yields $285 against a room that truly costs you $372 — so redeeming wins by $87, even though the headline cpp is bad. Judge the redemption against the currency you actually gave up.

The same discipline cuts the other way on earning: if your honest answer is "I cash them out," MR is a 0.6¢ currency and Gold's 4x dining is a 2.4% dining card with a $325 fee attached. Chase's 1.0¢ floor is far more forgiving of that.

2 — Compare against 2%, not against 1%. The relevant alternative is a no-fee 2% cash back card, so a 3x category at 1.5¢ (4.5%) is a real edge but a 1x category at 1.5¢ (1.5%) is a loss. Non-bonus spend belongs on the cash back card almost always.

3 — A cheap room is never a good award. Award prices move with cash prices in the dynamic programs, so redeeming on a $120 night locks in a bad cpp. Points are for the nights where cash pricing is absurd — peak dates, resort towns, conference weeks — and Hyatt's fixed chart is the one place that is reliably true.

What this page does not model

Signup bonuses (which usually dominate year one), authorized-user and referral points, elite qualification value, transfer bonuses beyond the field above, Amex Offers / Chase Offers, and the cash-flow reality that points are an interest-free loan to the issuer until you burn them.

Earn rates, annual fees, credits and transfer ratios change often, and several were restructured recently — every number on this page is an editable default, not a quote. Confirm current terms with the issuer or program before you act on any of it. Educational math only; not financial advice.
